Over the past month or so, I have been studying for the Offensive Security Certified Professional (OSCP) exam. The preparation process has taught me a lot about pentesting and shown a lot the weaknesses and strengths that I have in my skills. This post is the first in a series of monthly, OSCP-related updates that I will write leading up to my exam.




### Modules Completed:
- Vulnerability Scanning
- Intro to Web Application Attacks
- Common Web Application Attacks
- SQL Injections
- Client-Side Attacks
- Locating Public Exploits
- Fixing Exploits
- Password Attacks








## A story time in the application of the Dunning-Kruger effect
![Alt text](/assets/img/dunning.png)


As I started my OSCP studying journey, I focused heavily on the course book. Each section or module of the book is broken down into numerous learning exercises and practice labs that offer a way to get hands-on practice with concepts. The first couple of modules covered content that I had already learned in school, previous jobs, or throughout my own studies. I got used to completing learning module labs in 2-3 minutes and quickly started to chase the dopamine hit that I got after watching the course completion status increase percent by percent.


Without a doubt, I was starting to feel more and more confident in my capabilities. But as I continued to progress in the course, and as the content steadily became more difficult, I continued to have overconfidence in my skills until I eventually met my match and had to reevaluate my studying.


### Climbing to the Peak of "Mount Stupid" and diving into the "Valley of Despair"


My mindset shift happened as I was working on the SQL injection section. "SQL injection? How is there enough content to fill a whole section?" I scoffed to myself, opening the module. I had covered SQL injection in one course that I had taken 3+ years ago. I thought that I surely remembered enough about how it worked and what was possible with this attack path that I could blow through the module no problem.

![Alt text](/assets/img/feynman.jpg)


I was about halfway through the blind SQL injection subsection before I realized that the concepts weren't clicking, but I thought to myself, "Oh well, I'll power through it, and eventually all the pieces will come together." That was not the case. Each lab exercise only got more challenging. I found myself randomly inserting SQL queries in web app traffic with no real rationale, and when that didn't work, I was forming that habit of relying on hints and help from the offsec instructors. I was hitting a wall and losing the confidence that had propelled me through the first couple of sections. I had to change, and to do that I had to take ownership of failures as a learner. 




### The "Slope of Enlightenment"




Look, there's nothing wrong with getting help or guessing around to get the ball rolling and the thoughts flowing. My problem was with the fact that I wasn't digging deeper, I wasn't learning from my failures, and I wasn't allowing myself to struggle first before I asked for help. As I came to these realizations, I recognized that my first course correction was to re-read the book content and update my notes. As I went over my original notes, I realized how incredibly vague and incredibly BAD they were. My notes were one or two broken sentences and a couple of commands, so I threw them out and started over. More often than not, when I re-read the section, I ended up taking better notes and having a better understanding of the content (shocker, right?). As for the sections that I still had a hard time grasping, I either did more research on my own, or I asked for help from the course instructors. I didn't allow myself to write it off or trick myself into thinking I actually understood it.




### Embracing the "Try Harder" mindset




The course providers, Offsec, have a motto, and that motto is simply "Try Harder." As I have been studying for this exam, I had the perception of myself that I was always "Trying Harder." I know I am a hard worker. I know I have grit. But I also know that I am a human being and people can create a perception of themselves and their abilities that might not match reality. I thought that my dogged pursuit of completing numerous sections every day meant that I was trying harder. What that actually meant was, I gamified my studying and sacrificed my learning. I was willing to give up on problems so that I could keep making progress at the breakneck pace I had originally set. That wasn't growth. That wasn't learning. Most of all, that wasn't how I wanted to prepare for the OSCP exam.






![Alt text](/assets/img/Try-Harder-Components.png)




After taking the time to re-learn the module and hold myself accountable for my own understanding, I found that I was able to blitz through the lab exercises. My notes had become more detailed, my understanding of the concepts expanded, and my skills had improved. I understood then that I was finally living what it means to "Try Harder". Understanding the limitations of my knowledge and following through with a plan to address those limitations is trying harder. Having the ability to continuously take ownership of my mistakes and grow from them is trying harder.


I'm not perfect; I am still learning, and I am still developing my mindset as I approach my preparation for the OSCP exam. Going forward, I am going to continue to develop my skills, and most importantly, I am going to continue to "Try Harder."
