Over the past month or so, I have been studying for the Offensive Security Certified Professional (OSCP) exam and so far I have been loving it. I have gotten the chance to work on developing a lot of pentesting skills I already have in addition to creating new skills. The preparation process has taught me a lot about myself and areas that I can grow. While I can't talk specifics about course excercis, this post is meant to be the first in a series of OSCP-related updates that I use to document my progression towards earning the OSCP certification. Enjoy! 




### Technical Modules Completed:
- Vulnerability Scanning
- Intro to Web Application Attacks
- Common Web Application Attacks
- SQL Injections
- Client-Side Attacks
- Locating Public Exploits
- Fixing Exploits
- Password Attacks




## A story time in the application of the Dunning-Kruger effect
![Alt text](/assets/img/dunning.png)


As I started my OSCP studying journey, I focused heavily on the course book. 

In each section or module of the book there are learning exercises and practice labs. These excercise offer a way to get hands-on practice experimenting witht the concepts the book teaches. Throughout the first couple of modules I had found the excercises to be mostly review of concepts tha I had already learned in school, previous jobs, or my own studies. As a result I developed this false sense that the whole course would be like this. I quickly started to chase the dopamine hit that I got completing a module section and watching the course completion status increase percent by percent.


Without a doubt, I was starting to feel more and more confident in my capabilities. But as I continued to progress in the course, and the content steadily became more difficult, I continued to have overconfidence in my skills until I eventually met my match and had to shift how I approached preparing for the OSCP exam.


### Climbing to the Peak of "Mount Stupid" and diving into the "Valley of Despair"


My mindset shift happened as I was working on the SQL injection section (aobut 10 sections in, yikes). "SQL injection? How is there enough content to fill a whole section?" I remember thinking to myself as I started the module, little did I know section would take a whole week to progress through. In the past, I had covered SQL injection in a cyber course that I had taken 3+ years ago. I thought that I surely remembered enough about how it worked and what was possible with this attack path. I had no doubt that I could blow through the module and keep trucking along to the harder concepts in book.

![Alt text](/assets/img/feynman.jpg)


I was about halfway through the blind SQL injection subsection before I realized that the concepts weren't clicking like I thought they would. I thought to myself, "Oh well, I'll power through it, and eventually all the pieces will come together." That was not the case. Each lab exercise only got more challenging. I found myself randomly inserting SQL queries in web app traffic with no real rationale, and when that didn't work, I was relying on hints and help from the offsec instructors. I was hitting a wall and losing the confidence that had propelled me through the first couple of sections. I had to change, and to do that I had to take ownership of where I was failing in my learning. 




### The "Slope of Enlightenment"


Look, there's nothing wrong with getting help or guessing around to get the ball rolling and the thoughts flowing. My problem was with the fact that I wasn't digging deeper, I wasn't learning from my failures, and I wasn't allowing myself to struggle first before I asked for help. As I came to these realizations, I recognized that my first course correction was to re-read the book content and update my notes. As I went over my original notes, I realized how incredibly vague and incredibly BAD they were. My notes were one or two broken sentences and a couple of commands, that was it! So I decided to throw out the notes and started over. More often than not, as I re-read the section, I ended up taking better notes and having a better understanding of the content (shocker, right?). As for the sections that I still had a hard time grasping, I either did more research on my own, or I asked for help from the course instructors. I didn't allow myself to write it off or trick myself into thinking I actually understood it.




### Embracing the "Try Harder" mindset




The course providers, Offsec, have a motto, and that motto is simply "Try Harder." As I have been studying for this exam, I had the perception of myself that I was always "Trying Harder." I know I am a hard worker. I know I have grit. But I also know that I am a human being and people can create a perception of themselves and their abilities that might not match reality. I thought that my dogged pursuit of completing numerous sections every day meant that I was trying harder. What that actually meant was, I gamified my studying and in the pursuit of more XP I sacrificed my learning. I was willing to give up on problems so that I could keep making progress at the breakneck pace. That wasn't growth. That wasn't learning. Most of all, that wasn't how I wanted to prepare for the OSCP exam.






![Alt text](/assets/img/Try-Harder-Components.png)




After taking the time to re-learn the module and hold myself accountable for my own understanding, I found that I was able to blitz through the lab exercises that had once held me back. My notes had become more detailed, my understanding of the concepts expanded, and my skills had improved. I understood then that I was finally living what it means to "Try Harder". Understanding the limitations of my knowledge and following through with a plan to address those limitations is trying harder. Having the ability to continuously take ownership of my mistakes and grow from them is trying harder.


I'm not perfect; I am still learning, and I am still developing my mindset as I approach my preparation for the OSCP exam. Going forward, I am going to continue to develop my skills, and most importantly, I am going to continue to "Try Harder."
